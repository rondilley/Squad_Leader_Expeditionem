# RV-4: Legal and intellectual property status of a browser re-implementation of Squad Leader (1977)

Research date: 2026-09-07. Scope: United States law. Project: a non-commercial, open-source (GPL-3) browser edition of Avalon Hill Squad Leader.

This document is not legal advice. It is a research summary of public sources. Get an opinion from a lawyer before you make a decision that depends on it. Each legal statement has a source reference in square brackets. The References list at the end gives the URL for each reference. Verbatim legal text appears as preformatted text.

## Summary of results

- Hasbro owns the Avalon Hill copyrights and trademarks. Hasbro bought Avalon Hill from Monarch Avalon on 1998-08-04 for 6 million dollars [R1].
- Multi-Man Publishing (MMP) holds an exclusive license from Hasbro to make, distribute, and sell Squad Leader and Advanced Squad Leader products. The license dates from late 1998 [R2] [R3] [R4].
- The USPTO registration for the word mark SQUAD LEADER (Reg. 1102270, Avalon Hill, filed 1978) expired on 2009-05-16 [R5]. The USPTO cancelled the registration for ADVANCED SQUAD LEADER (Reg. 3981957, Hasbro, filed 2009) on 2018-01-26 for no Section 8 declaration of use [R6] [R7]. The registration for AVALON HILL (Reg. 2746862, Hasbro) is live [R8].
- US copyright law does not give protection to the idea of a game, its name, or its method of play. It does give protection to the text of the rules and to the artwork [R9] [R10] [R11].
- Courts hold that game rules and mechanics get no protection. The visual expression ("look and feel") and the artwork get protection [R12] [R13] [R14] [R15].
- The VASSAL project removes modules when a publisher tells it to. VASSAL makes module authors document the permission for third-party art [R16]. VASL (the ASL module) ships with the LGPL-2.1 license and no published permission statement from MMP or Hasbro [R17].
- The GPL-3 does not change the game IP question. It adds source-availability duties when you send JavaScript to browsers [R18] [R19].

## 1. Ownership chain and trademark status

### 1.1 Copyrights

Monarch Avalon sold the Avalon Hill Game Company to Hasbro on 1998-08-04 for 6 million dollars. The sale included "the rights to the Avalon Hill trademarks, copyrights, inventory, tooling and divisions" [R1]. Hasbro moved Avalon Hill to Wizards of the Coast in 2004, then back to Hasbro Gaming in January 2021 [R1]. Thus Hasbro owns the copyrights in the 1977 Squad Leader rulebook, maps, counters, and scenario cards. These sources show no subsequent sale of those copyrights.

### 1.2 MMP license

On 1999-02-08 Pyramid magazine published an MMP press release [R2]. MMP said: "During a flurry of face to face meetings late in the 1998 year we did in fact reach an agreement" [R2]. MMP also said: "MMP has an exclusive license to produce, distribute and sell the Squad Leader and Advanced Squad Leader gaming system" [R2]. GameSpot reported on 2000-04-27 that MMP had "an exclusive agreement with Hasbro Inc. to develop, produce, and distribute products for Avalon Hill's Advanced Squad Leader (ASL) game system, now owned by Hasbro" [R3].

The Squad Leader Academy status page says: "HASBRO has licensed Multi-Man Publishing the exclusive rights to the ongoing development of Advanced Squad Leader" [R4]. It adds: "In this same licensing agreement MMP has the exclusive rights to PREASL (SQL, COI, COD, GIA)" [R4]. The same page says that in January 2006 MMP agreed to put the Squad Leader rules online through the SQLA project [R4].

A BoardGameGeek forum thread contains a claim that the MMP license "did not include the rights to a computerized version" [R20]. The same claim says that MMP cannot "officially sanction" a computer-based version of the game [R20]. I could not open the thread to make sure of the poster or the date. Treat this claim as unverified community information.

Thus MMP and Hasbro can hold common-law rights in the names through continued sales.

Second, MMP and community sites print "Advanced Squad Leader is a trademark of Avalon Hill Games, Inc." [R24]. The names are in continuous commercial use. Treat them as marks with protection in practice.

## 2. US copyright doctrine on games

### 2.1 Statute and Copyright Office position

17 U.S.C. 102(b) [R9]:

```
In no case does copyright protection for an original work of authorship extend
to any idea, procedure, process, system, method of operation, concept,
principle, or discovery, regardless of the form in which it is described,
explained, illustrated, or embodied in such work.
```

The Copyright Office page "Games" says: "The idea for a game is not protected by copyright. The name or title given to the game and the methods for playing it are not protected by copyright." [R10]. It also says: "you may be able to register the text describing the rules of the game or the graphic art appearing on the gameboard or container" [R10].

Circular 33 says the Office "may, however, register a literary, graphic, or artistic description, explanation, or illustration of an idea, procedure, process, system, or method of operation" [R11]. It adds: "copyright protection will extend only to the original expression in that work and not to the underlying idea, methods, or systems described or explained" [R11]. It also says that names, titles, and short phrases are not copyrightable [R11]. It says: "the Office will not accept a claim to copyright in 'format' or 'layout'" [R11].

### 2.2 Cases

**Allen v. Academic Games League of America, 89 F.3d 614 (9th Cir. 1996)** [R12]. The court said "a copyright only protects a particular expression of an idea and not the idea itself." It applied the merger doctrine, which is "particularly applicable with respect to games 'since they consist of abstract rules and play ideas.'" It found that "Allen has not shown that it is possible to distinguish the expression of the rules of his game manuals from the idea of the rules themselves." It also held that "the playing of a game is not a 'performance' within the meaning of the Copyright Act." Result: rules and play ideas get no protection when the expression merges with the idea. Tournament play of a game is not a public performance.

**Spry Fox LLC v. LOLApps Inc., No. C12-147RAJ (W.D. Wash., order of 2012-09-18)** [R13]. The court examined a motion to dismiss. Judge Jones wrote that the idea of a hierarchical matching game is free to all: "Spry Fox's copyright gives it no monopoly over this idea." The judge then found protectable expression in the object hierarchy, the antagonist and helper objects, their visual depiction, and the setting.

On rules the judge wrote: "Game rules are a hybrid of the concept of the game and functional considerations." The judge also wrote: "the rules of the game are entitled to (at best) thin protection."

The order says creative language for rules "would be entitled only to protection against nearly identical copying" [R13]. Points and coins were scenes a faire [R13]. The court denied the motion on the copyright claim [R13]. The parties made an out-of-court agreement in October 2012 [R25].

**Tetris Holding LLC v. Xio Interactive Inc., 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012)** [R14]. The court said the rules of a game are "perforce in the public domain as well as the game itself." But expression gets protection "unless it is so inseparable from the underlying idea that there are no or very few other ways of expressing it." It found infringement in the copied visual elements. These were the piece shapes and colors, the 20 by 10 board, the ghost piece, the next-piece preview, the lockdown color change, and the game-over fill.

The court rejected merger because "there are many novel ways Xio could have chosen to express the rules of Tetris."

The court rejected scenes a faire because Tetris "is a wholly fanciful presentation" with no stock imagery [R14]. Result: summary judgment for Tetris Holding and an injunction [R14].

**DaVinci Editrice S.R.L. v. ZiKo Games LLC, 183 F. Supp. 3d 820 (S.D. Tex. 2016)** [R15] [R26].

The card game Legends of the Three Kingdoms copied the rules of Bang! with a new theme. The court found that "the method of play and rules common to the two card games did not support DaVinci's copyright-infringement claim."

The court said the rules "that make up a card-game system of play do not themselves produce the artistic or literary content that is the hallmark of protectable expression" [R26]. It found the settings, artwork, and written instructions "substantially different" [R15]. It granted summary judgment to the defendants [R15].

**Cases from 2020 to 2026.** I found no US court decision from 2020 to 2026 that changed this doctrine for board games. Secondary sources from 2023 to 2026 continue to cite DaVinci as the primary tabletop case [R26] [R27]. Chinese appellate courts reached the same result in 2024 and 2025 [R28].

The Wizards of the Coast v. Cryptozoic (Hex) case stopped with a private agreement in 2015 and no ruling on the merits [R29]. I removed each case that I could not read.

### 2.3 What the cases hold, by element

| Element | Protection | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Rules, mechanics, method of play, win conditions | None | [R9] [R10] [R12] [R15] |
| Numbers in tables that implement the mechanics | None, as a method of operation. See section 3(a) | [R9] [R11] |
| Rulebook prose | Thin. Protection against near-identical copies | [R13] [R12] |
| Board and counter artwork | Full, as pictorial works | [R10] [R14] |
| Look and feel: shapes, colors, and layout selections with many alternatives | Full | [R14] [R13] |
| Realistic or stock elements that the subject dictates | Scenes a faire, no protection | [R13] |
| Game name and title | No copyright. Trademark law applies | [R10] [R11] |

## 3. What this means in practice

### 3(a) Rules and tables in code

The Infantry Fire Table, the To Hit tables, and the unit values (firepower, range, morale) are the method of play. 17 U.S.C. 102(b) does not include a "procedure, process, system, method of operation" in copyright [R9]. Blank forms and layouts also get no protection [R11]. Thus you can write code that gives the same results as the tables. Write the tables as data in your own format.

Do not scan or copy the printed Quick Reference Data Card as an image. The printed card layout can have thin protection in its selection and configuration [R11]. A table with the same numbers in a new layout has the lowest risk that these sources show.

### 3(b) Rulebook text

Rulebook prose has thin protection. Spry Fox says creative rule language gets "protection against nearly identical copying" [R13]. Allen says rule expression can merge with the idea [R12]. Do not copy the 1977 or 2012 rulebook text into the project. Write the rules in your own words.

Keep the rule numbers from the rulebook, because numbers are not expression. Keep the technical terms (Prep Fire, Advancing Fire, Close Combat, Morale Check). Copyright does not give protection to short phrases [R11]. Do not ship a PDF of the rulebook.

### 3(c) Map art

The four mapboards are pictorial works with full protection [R10] [R14]. Do not reproduce the scanned boards. The hex grid, the terrain types, and the board dimensions are functional. The court in Tetris gave protection to the 20 by 10 board because "there are almost unlimited options" for that selection [R14]. A geomorphic board of 10 by 33 hexes with the same terrain layout has a risk, because the terrain layout is a creative selection.

New art "in the same style" has less risk, because style gets no protection. But the terrain positions can also count as expression. The path with the lowest risk is new boards that your code makes, plus new art. If you want the four Avalon Hill layouts, get a license from Hasbro or MMP.

### 3(d) Counter art

Counter art is pictorial work. The NATO-style symbols and the number layouts are functional and usual. Tetris gave protection to a fanciful presentation [R14]. Spry Fox found that realistic stock elements were scenes a faire [R13]. Draw new counters. Use the same numbers and the same positions for the numbers, because the positions communicate the values.

Do not copy the Avalon Hill silhouettes or the correct color values from the 1977 counters.

### 3(e) The project name

Copyright does not give protection to the name [R10]. Trademark law does. See section 5. Do not name the project "Squad Leader". Do not use a name that contains "Squad Leader" as the brand. Use a descriptive statement in the documentation, for example "an unofficial browser engine for the 1977 Avalon Hill Squad Leader rules".

### 3(f) Scenario titles and the order of battle

Titles are short phrases. Copyright does not give protection to them [R11]. An order of battle is a set of facts and a list of units. Copyright does not give protection to facts, and a "mere listing" gets no protection [R11]. The scenario card text and the scenario-specific rules prose have thin protection. Write scenario descriptions in your own words.

You can name the scenarios and give the same units, turn counts, and victory conditions.

## 4. Community precedent

### 4.1 VASSAL policy

The VASSAL documentation page "Module considerations" names three publishers: Games Workshop, Avalanche Press, and Sabertooth Games [R16]. VASSAL cannot post modules from them without explicit permission, "in accordance with the wishes of those publishers" [R16]. GMT Games has two conditions: "at least one player must own a physical copy of the game" and "modules may not be used for commercial use in any shape or form" [R16].

The page makes module authors add a "Copyright and License" section [R16]. That section records who owns the materials and what permission they gave [R16]. The page says that with no permission "it is illegal ... to distribute copyrighted materials" [R16].

A BoardGameGeek discussion of the policy says modules "will be pulled if a publisher requests it as a matter of course" [R30].

### 4.2 VASL

VASL is the ASL module for VASSAL. The vasl.info site says VASL "does not replace the need to own the boardgame" [R31]. The VASL source repository has the LGPL-2.1 license. Its README makes no statement about permission for the board or counter images from MMP or Hasbro [R17]. I found no published MMP or Hasbro permission statement for VASL. I also found no takedown record for VASL, for the SQLA rules project, or for other Squad Leader fan projects.

VASL started in the 1990s. Hasbro and MMP took no public action against it in that time. This is a record of tolerance, not a license.

### 4.3 Takedown history for board game projects

- Hasbro sent cease-and-desist letters and a DMCA notice for Scrabulous in 2008. Hasbro claimed that Scrabulous "infringed on Scrabble's copyrighted rules and appearance." Facebook removed the game [R32] [R33].
- Games Workshop removed Tabletop Simulator mods from Steam Workshop by DMCA notice in June 2026 [R34].
- Asmodee removed an Arkham Horror mod from Steam Workshop. Munchkin, Memoir 44, and Ticket to Ride mods "get taken down quickly," usually when the publisher has an official app [R35].
- Avalon Hill gave up the CIVILIZATION name to MicroProse in 1998. Avalon Hill moved all its CIVILIZATION trademark and board-game rights to MicroProse and paid 411,000 dollars [R36]. The value of the game name, not the mechanics, caused that litigation.

### 4.4 Open-source re-implementations that survived

- Freeciv is "inspired by" Sid Meier's Civilization. It ships with the GPL-2.0-or-later license. The web client has the AGPL-3.0-or-later license. The record shows no legal action against it [R37].
- OpenTTD re-implements Transport Tycoon Deluxe with the GPL-2.0 license. It used the 1995 game graphics until 2009, when OpenGFX gave "freely distributable graphics, sounds and music" [R38] [R39].
- FreeCol re-implements Colonization with the GPL license [R40].
- TripleA is a GPL-3 engine "with play similar to Axis & Allies and Risk." Its site compares play in descriptive text. It does not use the Axis & Allies name as a brand [R41].
- Battle for Wesnoth is new IP with the GPL-2+ and CC BY-SA 4.0 licenses [R42]. Ludii is a general game system with the CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 license [R43].

The pattern agrees across projects. Projects that re-implement the mechanics, use their own art, and do not use the trademark as a project name survive. Projects that copy the art or use the brand name on a competing product get takedowns.

## 5. Trademark: fair use, nominative use, and confusion

The USPTO says trademarks identify the source of goods and let owners "guard against counterfeiting and fraud" [R23]. The federal registrations for SQUAD LEADER and ADVANCED SQUAD LEADER are dead [R5] [R6]. But common-law rights come from use [R23], and MMP sells Squad Leader and ASL products today with a license [R2] [R24]. The AVALON HILL mark is live [R8].

Nominative fair use: New Kids on the Block v. News America Publishing, 971 F.2d 302 (9th Cir. 1992) [R44]. The court gave a three-part test:

```
First, the product or service in question must be one not readily identifiable
without use of the trademark; second, only so much of the mark or marks may be
used as is reasonably necessary to identify the product or service; and third,
the user must do nothing that would, in conjunction with the mark, suggest
sponsorship or endorsement by the trademark holder.
```

Descriptive fair use: 15 U.S.C. 1115(b)(4) [R45]. The defense covers this use:

```
a use, otherwise than as a mark, ... of a term or device which is descriptive of
and used fairly and in good faith only to describe the goods or services of
such party
```

Applied to the project:

- Statements such as "plays the 1977 Squad Leader rules" or "compatible with Squad Leader scenarios" satisfy the nominative test. You cannot identify the rule set without the name. Use plain text, not the Avalon Hill logo or typography. Add a notice that the project is not affiliated with, sponsored by, or endorsed by Hasbro or MMP.
- A project name such as "Squad Leader Online" or "Open Squad Leader" uses the mark as a brand for a competing game product. This fails the third part of the test and invites a confusion claim. Select a different name.
- Do not use "Avalon Hill" in the project name. The mark is live for board games [R8].
- The Spry Fox court dismissed the Lanham Act claim because "that consumer would likely recognize that different companies produced the two games" [R13]. A clear "unofficial" label and a different name support the same result.

## 6. GPL-3 interaction

The GPL-3 does not change the game IP analysis. The license covers only the code and assets that you own or that you have permission to license. It gives no right to third-party art or text.

Obligations when you ship browser JavaScript:

- GPL-3 section 1 says that "Corresponding Source" is "all the source code needed to generate, install, and (for an executable work) run the object code and to modify the work, including scripts to control those activities" [R18].
- Section 6 lets you convey a non-source form if you also give the source, for example by "network access to source at no additional charge" (option 6d) [R18].
- The FSF FAQ covers a website that contains or refers to GPL code "that are distributed to the user when they visit the web site" [R19]. In that condition "the source code for the programs being distributed must be released to the user under the terms of the GPL" [R19].
- The FSF recommends a license notice in the page that adds a permission with GPL-3 section 7 [R46]:

```
As additional permission under GNU GPL version 3 section 7, you may distribute
non-source (e.g., minimized or compacted) forms of that code without the copy of
the GNU GPL normally required by section 4, provided you include this license
notice and a URL through which recipients can access the Corresponding Source.
```

Practice: keep the unminified source in the public repository. Add the FSF-style license comment to the served HTML. Add a link to the repository in the served page.

AGPL for a LAN server. The GPL FAQ covers a person who runs modified GPL code on a server and does not supply copies to others. That person "does not have to release the modified sources" [R19]. The AGPL closes that hole [R19] [R47].

AGPL-3 section 13 makes a modified version "prominently offer all users interacting with it remotely through a computer network ... an opportunity to receive the Corresponding Source" [R48]. For a home LAN server that you run, the two licenses give the same result, because you publish the source.

AGPL applies only if a third party runs a modified server for others and keeps the changes private. Select AGPL-3 for the server if you want that protection. AGPL-3 section 13 lets you put AGPL and GPL-3 code together in one work [R48].

## Risk estimate and recommended posture

Risk level: low to moderate. The mechanics, tables, and rule numbers are safe to re-implement. This follows from 17 U.S.C. 102(b), the Copyright Office guidance, and the Allen, Spry Fox, and DaVinci cases. The three exposures are the 1977 map art, the 1977 counter art, and the use of "Squad Leader" as a brand.

VASL and the SQLA project show that Hasbro and MMP tolerated non-commercial fan software for decades. But tolerance is not a license. The Scrabulous case shows that Hasbro sends takedown notices when a fan product competes with an official digital product.

Recommended posture:

1. Give the project a different name. Refer to Squad Leader only in descriptive text with a non-affiliation notice.
2. Write all rules text and scenario text in your own words. Keep rule numbers and technical terms.
3. Encode the tables as your own data. Do not ship scans of the cards, the rulebook, or the boards.
4. Draw new counters and make new boards. Do not ship the four Avalon Hill board images. If you want them, send a written request to MMP or Hasbro.
5. Keep the project non-commercial. Do not sell it or accept payment for it.
6. Add a license notice and source link to the served page. Use AGPL-3 for the server if you want network-copyleft protection.
7. Record the permission status of each third-party asset in a COPYRIGHT file, as VASSAL does.
